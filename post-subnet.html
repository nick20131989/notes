Why /27 trips people up

Ask someone to range a /27 from memory and you will usually get two answers: one right, one that looks right.

A /27 gives 32 addresses. Eight of them exist, thirty-two use up the block, and the usable host range sits in the middle. The arithmetic is trivial; what fails is bookkeeping — which block you are actually in, and where the boundaries landed.

The two numbers that get mixed up

Block size versus usable hosts. A /27 is 32 addresses with 30 usable. People remember "30 hosts" and then step 30 through the address space, which drifts a little further off with every block. The step is 32. Hosts are what is left after the first and last address.

Network address versus the address you were given. If someone hands you 10.0.0.100/27, the network is not 10.0.0.100 — it is 10.0.0.96, because 100 sits inside the fourth block of 32. Everything downstream (broadcast, gateway choice, firewall rules) depends on getting that one right.

A ten-second check I actually use

Three steps, in this order, and the answer falls out:

  • Block size = 2(32 − prefix). /27 → 32.
  • Network = the address rounded down to a multiple of the block size (in the last octet, for anything /24 or longer).
  • Broadcast = network + block size − 1. Usable = everything between.

Doing that in my head at 32 addresses is fine. At /22 or with a non-octet boundary, I stop doing it in my head — a subnet calculator that prints the network, broadcast and usable range together is faster than being confidently wrong, and the printed range is something you can paste into a ticket.

The part that bites in production

  • A /27 sized for "30 hosts" that already has a gateway and a switch management address on it — so 28 usable, not 30.
  • Two teams allocating from the same /24 without a shared map, both landing in the same /27.
  • Overlapping ranges that only show up when the second site comes online.

None of these are subnetting mistakes. They are bookkeeping mistakes wearing subnetting clothes — which is why writing the range down (network, first host, last host, broadcast) matters more than the arithmetic.
